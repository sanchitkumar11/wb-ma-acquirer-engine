What the brief is hinting at

"The Healthcare Services sector has 46 transactions, with only ~12 in the $100–400M range. Strong candidates will think carefully about how to use adjacent sectors and the full dataset rather than filtering narrowly."

The ten values in the sector column are not peers. In a bank's healthcare coverage, healthcare services is the umbrella for businesses that deliver care and get paid by insurers or patients: physician practices, behavioral health, home health and hospice, dental service organizations, and facility operators. Health IT, devices, pharma and insurance are separate verticals with different economics. So the adjacency is the set of sub-sectors that work the way Healthcare Services works.

The numbers mostly agree with the business-model grouping

Why it matters for the valuation section

On the comp set ( closed control deals, provider services, $100–400M), the stated EV/EBITDA median is and the recomputed median is . On $15M of EBITDA that is versus . Whichever basis we pick has to be chosen on purpose and disclosed.

Could the stated multiples be forward multiples?

If the stated multiple used next year's EBITDA, the EBITDA it implies (deal size ÷ stated multiple) would sit a little above reported EBITDA, by roughly the growth rate, and the gap would track revenue growth. It doesn't.

  • The implied-to-reported ratio runs from to (5th to 95th percentile), median .
  • of rows would mean EBITDA is expected to shrink, in a file where every deal shows positive revenue growth.
  • The implied growth is unrelated to revenue growth (Spearman ρ = , p = ).
  • A synergy-adjusted EBITDA doesn't fit either: the gap is unrelated to synergy_pct_of_deal.

So the mismatch is noise, not a different basis.

Was it sent like that on purpose?

The file looks generated. A handful of relationships were clearly built in: sub-sector drives multiples, margins and deal size; each strategic sticks to one sub-sector; the Scale tag appears on every deal above $500M and on none below it; all carve-outs carry the Carve-out Complexity tag. Most other fields show no relationship to anything. The EBITDA mismatch fits that pattern: the multiple, the margin and the dollar EBITDA look drawn separately, each with its own noise. That reads as a by-product of the generator, not a planted trap.

Either way, the rubric scores data thinking at 15%, so catching it and handling it openly is what counts.

Should you clarify?

A short factual note is cheap and shows the catch early. The brief says they won't give hints on approach, so expect little back. Send it, don't wait on the reply, and state the assumption in the README. A draft is in our chat.

06

Is it supposed to match the real world?

What the brief says

"Acquirer names are real companies; target names are semi-fictional."
"The CSV is your only required data source. You may augment with public information (Wikipedia, SEC filings) but it is not expected or required."
Acquirer Overview: "Who they are: size, strategic priorities, recent M&A activity implied by the dataset."
"Would a VP send this to an MD without editing?"

My read

The CSV is the system of record for deal evidence and valuation. But the overview asks for the buyer's size and strategic priorities, and the file has no acquirer-size field. That part has to come from outside the CSV. And the VP test means the output can't contradict facts a banker knows, such as a buyer that's in bankruptcy or two names that are one company.

Proposed rule, three layers

  • Dataset facts carry all numbers and precedents, cited by transaction ID.
  • Public facts cover identity and status only (parent company, rename, bankruptcy, investment focus), sourced and labeled.
  • Never mix real deals into the precedent evidence, or state real fund sizes as numbers without a source. The file's deal histories are synthetic, so real ones would contradict them.

08

The 7/3 buyer mix, explained

The list has ten slots. The question is how many should go to strategics.

1. With no rule, sponsors take every slot

Rank all acquirers on one relevance score and all sponsors come before the first strategic, , at #. Each sponsor has 14–25 deals and the busiest strategic has 6. That's how the two populations appear in the file. It doesn't mean no strategic would buy this company.

2. A sell-side list wants both

Strategics may pay more because they can count synergies; sponsors bring depth and speed. Putting both on the list creates price tension. The guide's example process (p.57) started with 10–20 strategics and about 30 sponsors and ended with one of each competing for the deal.

3. Let comparable deals set the ratio

Rather than picking a number, look at who bought comparable companies. Provider-services deals of $100–400M: deals, by strategics () and by sponsors. That share of 10 slots is about 3. Private targets alone give the same answer ( of ), and so does Healthcare Services alone ( of ). That's the 7/3.

09

How we structure the engine, before the LLM

The stages run in this order. Each one has a decision we should settle together. The LLM layer starts at stage 9 and is a separate discussion.

  1. Load and check the file

    Read the CSV, run the reconciliation and field checks above, and record which basis each number uses.

    Decide: the canonical EV/EBITDA. Lean: stated as the headline, recomputed shown beside it, EV/Revenue as the cross-check since it's the only ratio that ties.

  2. Define the target in a config file

    Sector, EV, margin, ownership and region as inputs, never hardcoded. This also delivers the "arbitrary target" stretch goal.

    Decide: what "strong margins" means. Healthcare Services margins: median , 75th percentile , 90th . Lean: ~20%, which at implies ~$M EBITDA and ~$M revenue.

  3. Build the comp set

    Core plus ring 1, $100–400M, closed, control deals, weighted toward recent years. Produces the valuation ranges.

    Decide: ring weights and the time window. Lean: rings as above, weights tuned by the backtest, all years with recency weighting.

  4. Clean the buyer universe

    Start from all names, merge duplicates (Kindred at Home and CenterWell Home Health), flag status (Steward), and assign each to a lane.

    Decide: how much real-world overlay. Lean: a small, sourced file for identity and status only. No live web calls, which keeps runs fast and repeatable.

  5. Compute features per buyer

    Only from fields that passed the checks: sub-sector, buyer type, deal size, year, outcome, control flag.

    Decide: which dimensions from section 7 survive. Lean: relevance, size fit, activity and close record; drop the rest from scoring.

  6. Score and rank within each lane

    Transparent weights that a banker can read off the page, tested against the backtest.

    Decide: a strict 1–10 order or tiers. Because sponsors are indistinguishable here, a strict order among them would rank noise. Lean: tiers for sponsors, ranked order for strategics, with the reason written in the README.

  7. Fill the ten slots

    Apply the buyer-mix rule from section 8.

    Decide: option A, B, C or D. Lean: C with B's ratio.

  8. Set conviction

    Rule-based: score, depth of evidence (how many relevant precedents), and any status flags.

    Decide: the thresholds. Can wait until we see the score distribution.

  9. Assemble the evidence pack

    Everything the writing step is allowed to cite, per buyer. This is where the LLM layer begins.

    Next discussion.

  10. Evaluate

    Backtest: for each 2022–24 deal, rank buyers using only earlier deals and check whether the actual buyer makes the top 10.

    First probe ( deals): a simple sector-and-size score hits , the same as just listing the ten most active buyers (). Random picks would hit about . The flat result is the sponsor finding showing up again.

What this means for the design

For strategics, the file gives real signal: sub-sector identity and deal size. For sponsors, it can't separate the fifteen beyond deal count. So the structure should rank strategics, tier sponsors, let depth of evidence drive conviction, and keep a small labeled real-world layer for identity and status. Saying this plainly in the README is part of the data-thinking score.
